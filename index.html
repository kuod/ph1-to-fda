Method and caveats

What counts as a first-time approval

Drugs on FDA CDER's annual Novel Drug Approvals list (new molecular entities and new therapeutic biologics) from 21 October 2016 to 20 October 2026. 2021–2026 come straight from FDA's published tables. FDA has retired the 2016–2020 pages, so those years are rebuilt from Drugs@FDA through openFDA as original NDA/BLA approvals classed Type 1; annual counts land within one or two of CDER's published totals (2017 and 2020 are each one short for the CDER list), after de-duplicating same-day twin applications. CBER's first-time approvals are added alongside: cell and gene therapies, vaccines, and blood and plasma products (recombinant clotting factors, immune globulins and similar proteins), taken from CBER's product pages. CBER pages keep only recent approval letters, so first-approval dates for these were set by hand from FDA announcements and the pages supply the BLA number, manufacturer and indication. Albumin, fibrin sealants and hemostats, diagnostics and allergenics are not included, nor are new brands of an already-marketed active such as another albumin. The blood-product list leans toward new actives and routes, so it is a judgement call at the edges. CBER products carry no orphan or priority-review flags and no efficacy-supplement data, so they are left out of those statistics and out of the Buckets and Product Pipelines views. They are also left out of the funnel and the card hit rates: the big-sponsor crawl finds only a few of their programs, so counting their failures without their successes would pull the rates down. A card's approval count includes CBER products; its rates do not.

Sponsors and areas

Sponsors are rolled up to their parent as of October 2026 (for example Genentech to Roche, Celgene to BMS, Seagen to Pfizer). Therapeutic areas are assigned from the FDA indication text with keyword rules, with manual fixes where text was missing; a drug gets one area.

Phase history and "halted"

Every approved drug is traced through ClinicalTrials.gov by generic name, brand and code names (for instance MK-3475) found in the same intervention record. Only studies that started before approval are counted. A study is halted when its status is terminated, withdrawn or suspended. In Product Pipelines a condition is marked approved when it is mentioned in the product's current label, which is a keyword match and only a guide. Phase 1 trials are often not registered, so a drug can legitimately show no Phase 1.

The funnel

  • Programs are drug candidates identified in industry-sponsored Phase 1–3 studies that started 2008–2018 at 26 large sponsors. Aliases are merged when ClinicalTrials.gov lists them together (such as LY2439821 and ixekizumab). A program must carry one of its sponsor's own code prefixes (LY, PF, MK and so on) or match an FDA approval.
  • Programs whose active ingredient or brand was already FDA-approved before October 2016 are dropped, otherwise a 2012 approval would read as a failure. Vaccine, cell and gene therapy and blood-product programs are dropped for the CBER reason below.
  • The cohort is programs whose first Phase 1 started 2009–2018, giving at least eight years to reach approval. Phase 1/2 counts as Phase 2 and Phase 2/3 as Phase 3.
  • Halted: the last study at the highest phase reached was terminated, withdrawn or suspended. Stalled: studies finished and no later phase was registered, which usually means a quiet discontinuation. Active: something is still recruiting or running; these are left out of the success rates.
  • Success means a novel CDER approval inside this window. A drug approved only abroad, or filed but not yet approved, counts as not approved.

Known biases. Identification of failures depends on how a company labels its studies, so coverage differs widely and a thin sample leans toward its approved drugs. Alias fragmentation can make a program that was renamed after Phase 1 look like a Phase 1 failure, and a marketed or CBER product that slips past the filters reads as a failure. Pooled across the 26 sponsors this page shows about 42% Phase 1→2, 50% Phase 2→3, 60% Phase 3→approval and 13% overall; commonly quoted industry benchmarks are roughly 52%, 29%, 58% and 8%. So Phase 1 is likely understated, while Phase 2 and the overall hit rate are likely overstated because failures go uncounted. Treat company comparisons as indicative, and the pooled numbers as an upper-leaning estimate.

New indications, product pipelines and buckets

  • The Approvals and Who & What toggle adds drugs first approved before October 2016 that won at least one efficacy supplement in the window. Drugs@FDA labels supplements as Efficacy, Labeling or Manufacturing; efficacy ones carry new indications but also new populations and dosing, so they are a proxy for new indications, not an exact count.
  • Product Pipelines ranks products by efficacy supplements and, for the busiest 60, groups their industry-sponsored Phase 1–3 studies by condition. Conditions are folded into disease groups with keyword rules, so the grouping is approximate and one study can sit under several conditions.
  • Buckets split first-time approvals by approvals since launch: one and done (none later), one then a few (1–4), one then many (5–14), franchise (15+). Revenue on that plot is modelled without reference to the approval count, so it shows where blockbusters sit and not that more approvals cause more sales.

Revenue estimates

Central peak sales = median for the therapeutic area × orphan factor (0.5) × drug-type factor × priority-review factor (1.1) × sponsor-scale factor, scaled by 0.45, an informal calibration that has not been checked against reported totals. About 115 well-known drugs (37 of them CBER products) instead use an editorial peak taken from memory of public commentary. Annual sales follow an S-shaped ramp whose time to peak depends on area and orphan status, with the first year halved as a partial year, and four COVID products (two antivirals, two vaccines) use hand-set shapes. Low and high bands are 0.35× and 2.6×. These numbers are for exploration; they are not forecasts and not company-reported sales.
